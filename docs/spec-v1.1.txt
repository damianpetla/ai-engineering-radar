AI Engineering Radar
Product & Engineering Specification
Version: 1.1 Status: Ready for implementation Language: English (product and code) Product type: Public, evidence-based AI engineering trends observatory

1. Product Vision
AI Engineering Radar is a public, continuously updated observatory tracking how AI-related engineering practices, workflows, and techniques evolve over time.
Its primary purpose is to answer:
What's happening in AI engineering right now, and how is it changing?
The product is not a news portal, newsletter, recommendation engine, or technology adoption guide.
It should allow visitors to quickly understand:
Which engineering topics are receiving increased attention.
Which topics are becoming less visible.
Which topics have recently emerged.
How activity around individual topics has evolved.
What credible sources support the observations.
Whether evidence describes discussion, experimentation, or actual production use.
The experience must be visual, minimal, and immediately understandable.
1.1 Fundamental Principle
Observe. Do not recommend.
The radar must never imply that a popular topic is necessarily effective, valuable, or worth adopting.
Do not introduce:
Adopt / Trial / Assess / Hold classifications.
Editorial recommendations.
AI-generated adoption advice.
Best-practice rankings.
Subjective quality scores.
Unsupported claims about industry adoption.
Trend direction represents observed activity within the monitored source set, not engineering quality.
1.2 Target Audience
Software engineers.
Tech leads and engineering managers.
AI engineers.
Technical founders.
Engineering researchers.
People following AI-native software development.
The product is general-purpose and must not focus exclusively on Android, frontend, or any single technology stack.

2. Product Experience
2.1 One Dashboard
The public website should behave as a single, focused dashboard.
Avoid conventional navigation and multiple competing sections.
Visitors should immediately see the trends without needing to choose between News, Research, Topics, Reports, or other destinations.
The homepage is the product.
The visual direction is a modern, minimalist analytical dashboard rather than a technology news portal.
2.2 Homepage Structure
Suggested hierarchy:
Compact brand header.
Short product statement.
Date of the latest successful data update.
Small overview of monitored topics, signals, and independent sources.
Primary historical activity chart.
Compact, interactive topic list.
Inline topic details when selected.
Discreet methodology and project information in the footer.
Avoid oversized hero sections, promotional language, decorative dashboards, and unnecessary cards.
Suggested copy
AI Engineering Radar
The pulse of AI engineering.
Track how engineering practices evolve over time.
Evidence-based observations. No recommendations.
The wording may be refined during implementation.
2.3 Navigation
There must be no conventional main navigation menu.
Do not create top-level pages for:
News.
Articles.
Reports.
Recommendations.
Categories.
Research.
Methodology, source information, and project documentation may be available through unobtrusive footer links or accessible dialogs.
The main interaction must remain within the dashboard.
2.4 Main Chart
The primary chart should visualize how observed activity changes over time.
Requirements:
Multiple topic series.
Clear legend.
Interactive selection of visible topics.
Date-range selection, initially 30, 90, and 180 days.
Accessible tooltips.
Responsive layout.
Honest handling of incomplete historical coverage.
Explicit metric and unit labels.
Prefer a clean line chart.
Do not show more series simultaneously than can be understood.
Do not invent data to make charts appear populated.
2.5 Topic List
Below the chart, display a compact list of tracked topics.
Each row should contain:
Topic name.
Short category or description.
Small historical sparkline.
Observed change over the selected period.
Optional factual indication such as Rising, Stable, or Declining.
An affordance indicating that the row can be expanded.
Trend direction is descriptive, not evaluative.
Avoid green/red semantics suggesting good/bad.
Sorting and filtering should remain lightweight.
Reasonable options:
Highest recent activity.
Largest increase.
Largest decrease.
Recently emerged.
Alphabetical.
Do not introduce a complicated filter builder in the MVP.
2.6 Inline Topic Details
Clicking a topic should expand its details directly within the dashboard.
Do not navigate to a separate article-style page.
Expanded details should contain:
Neutral explanation of the topic.
Historical activity chart.
Relevant observed signals.
Original source links.
Publication and observation dates.
Evidence types.
Coverage limitations where relevant.
Examples of documented use when supported by sources.
The user should be able to close the expanded details without losing the dashboard context.
Prefer allowing one expanded topic at a time.
2.7 Shareable Topic State
Topic selection must be reflected in the URL.
Example:
/?topic=harness-engineering
Opening this URL should automatically expand the corresponding topic.
Closing the topic should update the URL.
Back and forward browser navigation must work.
Unknown topic identifiers must fail gracefully.
Topic identifiers must remain stable even if display names change.
2.8 Mobile-First Design
The dashboard must work exceptionally well on mobile devices.
Requirements:
No horizontal page scrolling.
No wide data tables.
Readable charts and labels.
Touch-friendly interactions.
Compact summary information.
Accessible expansion controls.
Good performance on mid-range phones.
On small screens, simplify chart labels and reduce visible series where necessary.
2.9 Visual Design
Default to a sophisticated dark interface, with accessible contrast and restrained use of accent colors.
Aim for:
Minimalism.
Clear information hierarchy.
Precise typography.
Subtle borders and surfaces.
Consistent spacing.
High information density without clutter.
Smooth but restrained interaction feedback.
Avoid:
AI-themed gradients everywhere.
Glowing orbs.
Excessive glassmorphism.
Decorative illustrations.
Animated backgrounds.
Generic SaaS landing-page patterns.
Dense navigation.
Unnecessary dashboard widgets.
The design should communicate analytical credibility.

3. Information Model
The system is built around four concepts:
Source.
Topic.
Signal.
TrendSnapshot.
3.1 Source
A Source identifies an original publisher or monitored information channel.
Suggested fields:
type Source = {
  id: string;
  name: string;
  url: string;
  type:
    | "engineering-blog"
    | "research"
    | "documentation"
    | "repository"
    | "technical-talk"
    | "other";
  organization?: string;
  independentGroup: string;
  enabled: boolean;
  discoveredAt: string;
};
independentGroup helps prevent counting multiple channels from the same publisher as independent evidence.
Sources should have stable identifiers.
3.2 Topic
A Topic represents a recognizable engineering technique, practice, workflow, or technical area.
Examples:
Context engineering.
Harness engineering.
Agentic coding.
Agent evaluation.
Multi-agent orchestration.
Coding-agent sandboxing.
AI-assisted code review.
Agent memory.
MCP-based tool integration.
AI-native testing workflows.
These are initial research candidates, not claims about current popularity.
Suggested model:
type Topic = {
  id: string;
  slug: string;
  name: string;
  description: string;
  category: string;
  aliases: string[];
  createdAt: string;
  status: "active" | "archived";
};
Topic descriptions must be factual and neutral.
Avoid duplicating semantically identical topics.
Topic taxonomy should evolve conservatively.
3.3 Signal
A Signal is a verified, source-backed observation associated with one or more topics.
Suggested model:
type Signal = {
  id: string;
  sourceId: string;
  canonicalUrl: string;
  title: string;
  publishedAt: string;
  observedAt: string;
  topicIds: string[];
  type:
    | "announcement"
    | "technical-explanation"
    | "independent-experiment"
    | "production-case"
    | "research-result"
    | "repository-activity";
  summary: string;
  originalEventId?: string;
  verificationStatus: "verified" | "review";
  evidenceNotes?: string;
};
A verified signal must have a real, retrievable primary source.
Summaries must accurately reflect the original material.
Do not infer production usage from a tutorial, announcement, or marketing claim.
3.4 TrendSnapshot
A TrendSnapshot stores aggregated measurements for a topic and a historical period.
Suggested model:
type TrendSnapshot = {
  topicId: string;
  periodStart: string;
  periodEnd: string;
  signalCount: number;
  independentSourceCount: number;
  productionCaseCount: number;
  coverageVersion: string;
  computedAt: string;
};
Snapshots should be reproducible from the underlying verified signals and the applicable methodology.

4. Measurement Methodology
4.1 What Is Being Measured?
The radar primarily measures observed activity in a curated set of credible sources.
It does not directly measure:
Actual market adoption.
Number of developers using a technique.
Effectiveness.
Technical quality.
Return on investment.
Community consensus.
The interface must communicate this distinction.
4.2 Core Metrics
Signal count
Number of unique verified signals associated with a topic during a given period.
Independent source count
Number of distinct independent source groups contributing relevant signals.
Production case count
Number of signals with documented production use.
This must be conservatively classified.
Production-case evidence should remain distinguishable from general discussion.
4.3 Trend Direction
Trend direction may be calculated from recent activity compared with a prior comparable period.
Use clearly documented rules.
Avoid misleading percentages when the baseline is zero or extremely small.
For example, when a topic increases from zero to several observations, label it as newly observed rather than displaying an infinite percentage increase.
Distinguish between:
Rising activity.
Declining activity.
Relatively stable activity.
Newly observed activity.
Insufficient data.
Do not interpret these labels as recommendations.
4.4 Historical Coverage
A topic may have existed long before the radar began monitoring it.
Do not treat the first observed signal as the invention date of the technique.
Historical charts must distinguish between:
A period with observed zero activity.
A period for which collection coverage is incomplete or absent.
Missing data is not zero.
4.5 Source Independence
Multiple articles describing the same original announcement must not be counted as independent evidence of separate events.
Use:
Canonical URLs.
Publisher identity.
Original event identifiers.
Similarity checks.
Source relationships.
Do not artificially inflate trends through syndication.
4.6 Methodology Transparency
Provide a concise, accessible explanation of:
What is monitored.
How sources are selected.
How signals are verified.
How duplicates are handled.
What trend metrics mean.
What the measurements cannot establish.
The methodology must be available without dominating the homepage.

5. Source Strategy
5.1 Initial Source Categories
Monitor a balanced mix of:
First-party engineering blogs.
AI research organizations.
Developer-tool engineering teams.
Independent technical practitioners.
Relevant open-source repositories.
Technical documentation and research publications.
Potential sources include:
OpenAI engineering publications.
Anthropic engineering publications.
Google DeepMind.
Cursor.
Sourcegraph.
Simon Willison.
Mitchell Hashimoto.
Armin Ronacher.
Relevant GitHub projects.
These are candidates, not automatically approved feeds.
Verify actual feeds, endpoints, access conditions, and publication relevance.
5.2 Source Selection Criteria
Prioritize:
Original technical information.
Demonstrable engineering experience.
Concrete implementation details.
Reproducible experiments.
Transparent methodology.
Production engineering reports.
Deprioritize:
Rewritten announcements.
SEO-driven AI content.
Promotional newsletters.
Affiliate marketing.
Unsupported predictions.
Content without attributable sources.
A source can be credible while an individual claim remains weak.
5.3 Coverage Diversity
Avoid allowing one organization, platform, or highly active publisher to dominate the dataset.
Expose source concentration where useful.
Changes in the source roster must be tracked because they affect comparability over time.

6. Automated Data Pipeline
The radar should have an independent ingestion pipeline.
Do not depend on ChatGPT's personal scheduled reports as the source of website data.
6.1 Pipeline Stages
Discover candidate publications.
Fetch permitted source content.
Extract structured observations.
Verify source and factual claims.
Deduplicate publications and original events.
Match observations to existing topics.
Flag uncertain classifications for review.
Persist verified signals.
Recompute affected trend snapshots.
Validate and publish the updated dataset.
6.2 Discovery
Prefer stable RSS, Atom, official APIs, and other permitted public interfaces.
Respect rate limits and access restrictions.
Use bounded fetching and retries.
Do not bypass paywalls or authentication requirements.
6.3 AI-Assisted Extraction
An LLM may help with:
Topic identification.
Structured summaries.
Evidence-type classification.
Duplicate detection.
Candidate topic discovery.
However, LLM output is untrusted.
Validate against strict schemas.
Do not allow the model to invent:
URLs.
Publication dates.
Quotations.
Source identities.
Production deployments.
Historical measurements.
Keep prompts and model configuration versioned.
6.4 Verification
Before publishing a signal, verify:
The original URL exists.
The source is identifiable.
Publication timing is sufficiently grounded.
The extracted summary is supported.
Topic mapping is reasonable.
Evidence classification is justified.
The observation is not a duplicate.
Unverifiable signals should be excluded from public metrics.
6.5 Human Review
The pipeline should minimize routine manual work.
Allow automatic publication when validation is sufficiently reliable.
Route ambiguous cases to review, especially:
Newly discovered topics.
Uncertain production claims.
Conflicting publication dates.
Suspected duplicate events.
Unclear original attribution.
A simple review file or pull-request workflow is sufficient for the MVP.
Do not build a full administration panel unless necessary.
6.6 Idempotency
Repeated pipeline execution must not duplicate signals.
Use stable IDs and canonical source identifiers.
Pipeline runs should be reproducible and produce meaningful logs.
6.7 Scheduling
Use GitHub Actions or an equivalent managed scheduler.
Initial target: run every three days.
Provide manual execution for debugging and backfills.
Failure must not overwrite the latest valid public dataset.

7. Historical Backfill
The initial public dashboard should contain enough real historical information to make trend charts meaningful.
7.1 Requirements
Collect historical publications from verifiable sources.
Preserve actual publication dates.
Track source coverage.
Deduplicate events.
Clearly mark incomplete historical periods.
Do not generate synthetic historical measurements.
7.2 Scope
Aim for up to six months of historical coverage where reliably available.
Do not force a six-month history if sources do not support it.
A smaller, well-grounded dataset is preferable to a larger unreliable one.
7.3 Initial Dataset
Target approximately 10–15 meaningful topics, selected based on actual observed evidence.
Do not create artificial activity simply to populate the UI.
The implementation may include development fixtures for testing, but these must never be presented as production observations.

Static-first.

Low operational cost.
Minimal infrastructure.
Reproducible data.
Explicit schemas.
Good test coverage.
Easy local development.
Clear separation of ingestion and presentation.

Astro.
React for interactive dashboard components.
A lightweight charting library.
Zod for validation.
Vitest for unit and integration tests.
Playwright for end-to-end tests.
GitHub Actions.
Cloudflare Pages or an equivalent static hosting service.
Codex may adjust library choices when justified.

8.4 Data Storage
Prefer version-controlled structured files for the MVP.
Advantages:
Transparent history.
Simple deployment.
No database operations.
Reviewable changes.
Easy rollback.
Use a consistent serialization format and validate all persisted records.
Avoid storing redundant derived data when it can be safely recomputed.
8.5 Frontend Architecture
Separate:
Data access.
Trend aggregation and calculations.
Presentation components.
URL state management.
Accessible interaction primitives.
Keep the dashboard fast and resilient.
Do not put source extraction or AI calls in the browser.

9.1 Testing
Implement tests for:
Schema validation.
Canonical URL normalization.
Deduplication.
Source independence.
Topic matching.
Trend calculations.
Zero-baseline handling.
Missing historical coverage.
Data serialization.
URL-driven topic selection.
Dashboard interactions.
Responsive behavior.
9.2 Accessibility
Support:
Keyboard navigation.
Semantic controls.
Visible focus.
Accessible chart summaries.
Appropriate contrast.
Screen-reader-friendly trend descriptions.
Reduced-motion preferences.
9.3 Performance
Aim for excellent Core Web Vitals.
Avoid shipping large datasets unnecessarily.
Prefer static generation and lightweight client-side interactivity.
Charts must remain usable on mobile.
9.4 Error Handling
The public website must gracefully handle:
No available observations.
Partial historical coverage.
Missing topic details.
Invalid URL parameters.
Temporarily stale data.
Data-loading errors.
The pipeline must report failures clearly.
Do not silently publish incomplete or invalid updates.
9.5 Security
Keep API keys in deployment secrets.
Do not expose model credentials to the browser.
Treat external content as untrusted.
Validate generated structured data.
Apply reasonable timeouts and request limits.
Minimize third-party dependencies.
Use least-privilege CI/CD credentials.

10. Deployment
The objective is a real, publicly accessible production website.
10.1 Requirements
Git repository.
Automated build and validation.
Production hosting.
HTTPS.
Automated deployment.
Scheduled data collection.
Clear deployment documentation.
Basic error monitoring or failure notifications.
10.2 Preferred Approach
Use GitHub for repository and automation.
Use Cloudflare Pages or another appropriate static hosting provider.
Prefer free or low-cost infrastructure when practical.
Do not incur paid costs without user approval.
10.3 Deployment Process
Codex should:
Implement and test the project locally.
Configure the production build.
Create or configure the repository with permission.
Configure CI workflows.
Configure hosting with permission.
Set required secrets through secure interfaces.
Deploy the website.
Verify the public URL.
Test the dashboard on desktop and mobile.
Verify a complete ingestion and publication cycle.
Document maintenance and recovery procedures.
Where external access is unavailable, provide exact steps and resume when the user completes them.
Never claim deployment succeeded without verification.

13. Definition of Done
The project is complete when:
A visitor can open a public URL and immediately understand current observed AI engineering activity.
The homepage is a minimalist dashboard without a conventional navigation menu.
Visitors can compare historical activity across topics.
Clicking a topic expands evidence-backed details inline.
Individual topics have shareable URLs.
Every published observation links to an authentic source.
Metrics are reproducible and their limitations are explained.
The website never confuses popularity with effectiveness.
Historical charts do not contain fabricated data.
The ingestion pipeline runs automatically.
Tests and CI/CD pass.
The production deployment has been verified.
The project can be maintained without substantial manual intervention.

14. Instructions for the Implementing Agent
Codex should treat this document as the primary product specification.
Work autonomously on routine implementation decisions.
Favor simplicity over speculative flexibility.
Do not add functionality simply because it is common in dashboards or SaaS products.
Preserve the product's defining characteristics:
One dashboard. Historical trends. Verifiable evidence. No recommendations.
Proceed milestone by milestone and keep the repository functional.
Ask for user input only when genuinely necessary, including:
Credentials.
External permissions.
Paid infrastructure.
Domain ownership.
Major product trade-offs.
The final goal is not a prototype or architecture proposal.
The final goal is a deployed, operational AI Engineering Radar.
